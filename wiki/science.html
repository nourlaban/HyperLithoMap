Scientific Background

This page explains the physical principles behind hyperspectral remote sensing and its application to geological mapping — the scientific foundation of every step in the HyperLithoMap pipeline.

◆ What is Hyperspectral Imaging?

Conventional colour cameras record three broad bands (red, green, blue). Multispectral sensors extend this to 4–12 bands. Hyperspectral sensors record hundreds of narrow, contiguous spectral bands across the entire optical range — typically 400–2500 nm — effectively delivering a full spectrum at every pixel of the image.

The electromagnetic spectrum showing the PRISMA and EnMap sensor coverage (400–2500 nm).

Key advantage: The 234 bands in PRISMA data give a spectral resolution of ~8–10 nm per band. This resolves fine absorption features (widths of 20–80 nm) that are diagnostic of specific minerals — features that are completely smeared out in Landsat or Sentinel-2 multispectral data.

◆ Spectral Signatures of Geological Materials

Every mineral absorbs electromagnetic radiation at wavelengths determined by the electronic and vibrational properties of its crystal structure. These absorption features produce characteristic dips in the reflectance spectrum — the mineral's spectral fingerprint.

Schematic reflectance spectra for four Rabshi geological units. Note the Fe²⁺ absorption near 1000 nm in mafic rocks and the Al–OH absorption near 2200 nm in alteration zones.

◆ The Machine-Learning Challenge

Classifying hyperspectral data is harder than conventional image classification for four reasons:

  • High dimensionality (Hughes Phenomenon): With 234 bands and only hundreds of training samples per class, classical distance-based methods become unreliable. Dimensionality reduction (implicit in deep models) or regularised classifiers (RF, XGB) are required.
  • Spatial–spectral coupling: Adjacent pixels of the same rock type share spectral similarity AND spatial texture. Models that process both jointly (S²Mamba, SpectralFormer) outperform purely spectral approaches.
  • Mixed pixels: At 30 m resolution, a pixel near a geological contact contains contributions from multiple units. Spatial context helps resolve these ambiguous pixels.
  • Label scarcity: Field mapping is expensive; training sets are small relative to image size. Class-weighted loss and regularisation are essential.
Critical implementation note: The PRISMA Rabshi scene contains ~11% nodata pixels (value −9999) at image boundaries. These must be excluded from per-band min–max normalisation — otherwise all valid spectral values compress to ~1.0, erasing inter-class discrimination and causing model collapse. See hsmodel/utils.py load_HSI() for the fix.
